the 2026–27 edition

What we’re working on this year

Attention

Children are growing up inside an economy engineered to consume their attention. We work on winning it back with the lesson itself: starting from a real question instead of a ready answer, gathering a class without raising your voice, building motivation that doesn’t run on grades.

AI

Not another “grade homework with ChatGPT” course. Each cohort brainstorms what AI changes in its lessons, drawing on our team’s years in tech and AI education, then tests the three to five best ideas with children in the practice weeks.

How the program works

  1. 1

    Intensive

    We start with what the job is for and what has changed this year. Then we take practical teaching methods apart, try them on each other, and see what happens.

  2. 2

    Clubs

    We organize children’s clubs and gather the groups: over half the program happens there. For several weeks you lead sessions with real children, where the stakes are low and getting it wrong is allowed.

  3. 3

    Mentor

    Every week, in a small group, you go over your sessions with a mentor: what went off plan and what to change next time.

  4. 4

    Your class

    What works, you bring back to your own classroom. That is what the whole program is for.

the content changes every year. the structure doesn’t.

Coordinators go through the same four steps; their practice is real cases from their own schools rather than clubs.

Teacher program pilot Summer Camp Tbilisi

Tbilisi, Georgia1.07–1.09.202617 teachers finished the intensive

The first intensive focused on one tool, inquiry lessons. We surveyed the teachers after it, and again after several weeks of practice with a mentor.

  • 60%→25% of teachers worried about adapting when a lesson goes off plan
  • 40%→81% of answers on the fundamentals of an inquiry lesson at “I understand it and can use it”
  • 8.8→9.5 average recommendation score, out of 10
  • 3 in 4 children surveyed would like lessons like these at school

The first pilot

Teachers’ responses changed noticeably after practice. On the fundamentals — what an inquiry lesson is for, how it differs from a merely engaging one, and what the teacher does — the share of answers at “I understand and can use it” rose from 40% to 81%. Fewer teachers reported being worried about having to adapt when a lesson went off plan.

Not everything became easier. Group dynamics remained difficult, and we’re adding more practice and mentoring around it.

Encouraging results, but still from a small group in an unusually friendly setting. We’ll see how they hold up in regular classrooms.

on our desk

Why curiosity, and why before 15

In PISA 2025, Israeli 15-year-olds said they try as hard as anyone. Yet on tasks that counted for nothing, they stopped about twice as often as their OECD peers. So we work with teachers of younger classes, before that sets in. And in Israel, curiosity goes with science scores as strongly as family background does, which is why our lessons start from it.

Read our PISA 2025 note

PISA also asked about curiosity. Israeli students report a little less of it than their OECD peers: 68% say they are curious about many different things, against 73% across the OECD. And curiosity goes with results.

morim foundationResearch note · PISA 2025 PISA 2025: Israeli students don’t just score lower – they give up sooner The September headlines were about the averages. We went back to the OECD and RAMA reports, and to the raw data, to see what the averages hide – and what it means for teaching. By the self-reported numbers, Israeli students look like everyone else: 59% say they put in extra effort when work gets hard, against 60% across the OECD. In practice, they don’t. Israeli students gave up on 14% of tasks in the digital test (OECD: 7%); 34% stopped answering the questionnaire before its later sections (OECD: 15%); and 19.7% of their reading answers were fast and wrong (OECD: 8.9%). On all three measures, Israel is first in the OECD. so we start earlier

FAQ

Who is the Fellowship for?
Practicing teachers in Israel who teach the later years of primary school or the first years of middle school, and who want to keep changing how they teach. If you work with another age group, get in touch anyway and tell us about your experience.
Is this an inquiry-based learning program?
Inquiry is one of our tools, and the sharpest one we know for building curiosity and intrinsic motivation. But it is slow and it isn’t for every lesson, so we also teach when not to use it.
What about AI?
It is one of this year’s two themes, and we don’t treat it as a tool course. Each cohort works out what AI changes in its own lessons and tests the best ideas with children. Our position: teachers who use AI and teach children to use it, not AI in place of teachers.
Do participants receive a stipend?
Yes. Both teachers and coordinators receive a stipend. The amount and payment arrangements will be provided with the details of each program.
Can a school or municipality work with Morim?
Yes. We’re looking for schools, networks and municipalities that want our programs for their teachers or coordinators, and for experienced educators who can help us build them. Read about partnering with us.
